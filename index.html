Stage 1 • George Washington's Presidency

🏛️ Setting presidential precedents

George Washington became the first U.S. president in 1789. Because the office was new, his actions helped establish traditions that later presidents could follow or change.

Cabinet precedent

Washington relied on department heads such as Thomas Jefferson and Alexander Hamilton for advice. The Constitution does not use the word “cabinet,” but Washington's practice helped establish the presidential cabinet.

Two-term tradition

Washington voluntarily left office after two terms. This created a powerful precedent that lasted until Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected to four terms; the 22nd Amendment later limited presidents to two elected terms.

Peaceful transfer

Washington's retirement demonstrated that political power could be transferred without a president holding office permanently.

Advisor decision

Stage 2 • National Debt & Hamilton

💰 The new nation has debt

The United States inherited significant Revolutionary War debt. Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton proposed a financial program that included federal assumption of state debts, federal funding of the debt, tariffs, and an excise tax on distilled spirits.

Federalist approach

Support a stronger national government, federal management of public credit, a national bank, manufacturing, commerce, and financial policies that could strengthen the federal government.

Jeffersonian/Anti-Federalist tradition

Favor a more limited federal government, stronger state authority, an agricultural republic, and suspicion of concentrated financial power.

Policy choice

Key debate: Federalists and their opponents disagreed over national debt, taxation, the national bank, and the appropriate size and power of the federal government.
Stage 3 • Whiskey Rebellion

🥃 Can the new government enforce its laws?

In 1791 Congress imposed an excise tax on distilled spirits. Farmers in western Pennsylvania and elsewhere strongly resisted the tax. The conflict became known as the Whiskey Rebellion.

In 1794 Washington called militia forces from several states and personally traveled toward Pennsylvania. The rebellion collapsed without a major battle.

Why it mattered: The federal government demonstrated that it had the power to enforce a law passed by Congress. Washington's response also demonstrated the power of the executive branch to use federal authority to uphold federal law.

Constitutional choice

Stage 4 • Foreign Policy

🌎 Washington and avoiding permanent alliances

Europe was at war during Washington's presidency. Americans were divided over whether the United States should support revolutionary France, Britain, or remain neutral.

Washington's administration issued the Proclamation of Neutrality in 1793. In his Farewell Address (1796), Washington warned against permanent alliances and urged Americans to avoid unnecessary political attachments to foreign powers.

Why neutrality?

The young United States was militarily and economically vulnerable. Washington believed the country should have room to grow rather than become trapped in European wars.

France vs. Britain

Some Americans supported revolutionary France because France had helped the United States during the Revolution. Others favored Britain because of trade and economic connections.

Decision

Farewell Address connection

Washington's Farewell Address discussed dangers of permanent alliances and partisan divisions. It was advice rather than a constitutional amendment or law.

Stage 5 • John Adams

🦅 The Adams presidency

John Adams became the second president in 1797. His presidency was dominated by foreign tensions with France, domestic political conflict, and disagreements within the Federalist Party.

The XYZ Affair

French officials demanded concessions from American diplomats. When the American government reported the incident using the letters X, Y, and Z, public anger toward France increased.

Adams ultimately avoided a full-scale war with France through diplomacy, even though many Federalists wanted a tougher response.

Political consequence

Stage 6 • Alien & Sedition Acts

📰 The Alien and Sedition Acts

In 1798, the Federalist-controlled Congress passed several laws known collectively as the Alien and Sedition Acts. They increased federal power over immigrants and restricted certain forms of speech critical of the government.

ActBasic effect
Naturalization ActLengthened the residency requirement for citizenship.
Alien ActsExpanded presidential authority involving certain noncitizens.
Sedition ActCriminalized certain false or malicious writings and speech against the federal government.
Political effect: Democratic-Republicans argued the laws threatened free speech and federal power was being abused. Federalists defended them as measures connected to national security and political stability. The controversy hurt the Federalists politically and became an important issue in the election of 1800.

Historical thinking

Stage 7 • Election of 1800

🗳️ The election that exposed a constitutional problem

The election of 1800 was intensely contested. Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr, running on the same Democratic-Republican ticket, each received the same number of electoral votes.

Under the Constitution as it then operated, the election went to the House of Representatives. The House voted repeatedly before Jefferson finally won the presidency, with Burr becoming vice president under the system then in place.

Why this mattered: The election revealed a flaw in the original Electoral College system. The 12th Amendment, ratified in 1804, required electors to cast separate votes for president and vice president.

Decision

Stage 8 • Thomas Jefferson

🌾 Jefferson's vision of government

Jefferson and his Democratic-Republican allies generally favored a more limited federal government, lower taxes, agricultural interests, and stronger state authority. Jefferson also believed the Constitution should be interpreted more strictly than Federalists such as Hamilton generally preferred.

Federalist tendencies

Stronger national government • commerce and manufacturing • national financial institutions • broader interpretation of federal powers • closer economic ties with Britain.

Jeffersonian tendencies

Limited federal government • agriculture • stronger state role • strict interpretation • concern about concentrated financial and political power • sympathy for revolutionary France.

Stage 9 • Louisiana Purchase

🗺️ Jefferson faces a constitutional dilemma

In 1803, the United States agreed to purchase the Louisiana Territory from France for $15 million. The purchase roughly doubled the territory of the United States.

Why did Napoleon sell?

Napoleon Bonaparte needed money and resources for his European ambitions. France had also suffered the loss of Saint-Domingue (Haiti) after the Haitian Revolution, weakening French plans for a major American empire. The renewed war with Britain also made maintaining a large North American territory less attractive.

Why did Jefferson want it?

Control of the Mississippi River and New Orleans was strategically important. Jefferson also saw western land as important to an agricultural republic.

The constitutional problem

Jefferson generally favored strict interpretation, yet the Constitution did not explicitly state that the president could purchase a huge foreign territory. Jefferson therefore faced a tension between his constitutional philosophy and his policy choice.

Jefferson's choice

Historical debate: Jefferson privately considered whether a constitutional amendment might be needed, but ultimately proceeded with the purchase through a treaty and related legislation.
Stage 10 • Lewis, Clark & Sacagawea

🧭 Explore the new territory

Jefferson sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark to explore the newly acquired territory, map routes, study geography and natural resources, and establish relationships with Native peoples.

Sacagawea, a Lemhi Shoshone woman traveling with the expedition, served as an interpreter and cultural intermediary. Her presence helped the expedition communicate and travel through unfamiliar regions. Her husband, Toussaint Charbonneau, also traveled with the expedition.

The expedition's job

  • Map rivers and geography.
  • Record plants, animals, and natural resources.
  • Look for routes, including a practical water route to the Pacific.
  • Establish diplomatic relationships with Native nations.
  • Gather information about the new territory.

Expedition decision

Stage 11 • Barbary Pirates

⚓ Jefferson and the Barbary States

North African states along the Barbary Coast had demanded payments, often called tribute, from foreign governments in exchange for protection of merchant shipping. American merchant ships became involved in these conflicts after independence.

Jefferson initially tried diplomacy, but during the First Barbary War (1801–1805), the United States used naval forces and Marines against Tripoli after Tripoli declared war on the United States.

Why this mattered: Jefferson's administration demonstrated that even a president who favored limited government could use federal military power when he believed American commerce and national interests required it.

Decision

Stage 12 • Midnight Judges

⚖️ The last days of the Adams presidency

After the election of 1800, President Adams and the Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Judiciary Act of 1801, creating new federal judicial positions. Adams filled many of these positions near the end of his presidency.

Because these appointments occurred late in Adams's term, Jefferson's supporters called some of the appointees the “midnight judges.”

Political conflict: Jefferson's supporters saw the appointments as an attempt by Federalists to retain influence in the judiciary. The incoming administration sought to prevent some commissions from being delivered.

Why did this become a constitutional issue?

Stage 13 • Marbury v. Madison

⚖️ Marbury v. Madison (1803)

William Marbury was one of Adams's judicial appointees whose commission was not delivered. He asked the Supreme Court to order Secretary of State James Madison to deliver it.

Chief Justice John Marshall wrote the Court's decision. The Court concluded that part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 conflicted with the Constitution because it attempted to give the Supreme Court authority beyond what Article III allowed.

Why it matters: Marbury v. Madison is associated with the principle of judicial review—the authority of courts to declare a law inconsistent with the Constitution.

Final constitutional question
